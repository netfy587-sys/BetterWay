01

Origins and history

Basketball is one of the few major sports with a known inventor, a known date, and a known reason for existing. It was built to solve a specific, practical problem — and it spread faster than almost any game before it.

In December 1891, physical education instructor James Naismith was asked to invent an indoor game to keep athletes at the International YMCA Training School in Springfield, Massachusetts, active during the cold New England winter. He needed something that avoided the rough tackling of football but still demanded skill and team play. His solution used a soccer ball and two peach baskets nailed to the elevated running track at either end of the gymnasium, along with thirteen basic rules he posted on the wall.

Because the baskets kept their bottoms, someone had to climb a ladder to retrieve the ball after every score, until metal hoops and open nets replaced the baskets a few years later. The name "basketball" was suggested by one of Naismith's own students.

Today the sport is governed internationally by FIBA (the International Basketball Federation), while national leagues such as the NBA, WNBA, EuroLeague, and CBA (China) run the sport's biggest professional competitions.

02

How the game is played

Strip away the strategy and basketball is simple: get the ball into the opponent's hoop more times than they get it into yours, within a set amount of time, without breaking the rules of contact and ball handling.

Teams and objective. Two teams of five players each (with substitutes on the bench) try to score by shooting the ball through the opponent's elevated hoop. The team with more points when time expires wins.

Scoring. A basket made from inside the three-point line is worth two points. A basket made from beyond the three-point line is worth three points. A free throw, awarded after certain fouls, is worth one point.

Game structure. The NBA plays four 12-minute quarters (48 minutes total). International FIBA games and the WNBA use four 10-minute quarters. college basketball in the United States uses two 20-minute halves. Overtime periods are played if the score is tied when time expires.

Moving the ball. Players can advance the ball only by dribbling (bouncing it while walking or running) or passing it to a teammate. Running with the ball without dribbling is called traveling, and stopping a dribble and then starting it again is called a double dribble — both are violations that hand possession to the other team.

The shot clock. A team must attempt a shot within a fixed time after gaining possession — 24 seconds in the NBA and FIBA competition — or possession passes to the other team. This rule was introduced to stop teams from simply holding the ball to protect a lead.

Fouls. Personal fouls cover illegal contact such as pushing, holding, or blocking a player's path illegally. A player who commits too many fouls in a game is disqualified ("fouls out"). Fouls committed against a player who is shooting typically result in free throws.

Common violations at a glance: traveling (walking with the ball), double dribble (dribbling, stopping, then dribbling again), goaltending (illegally interfering with a shot on its way down), and backcourt violation (a team returning the ball to its own half after crossing midcourt).

03

The court and equipment

Every marking on a basketball court has a purpose — each line defines a rule, a scoring zone, or a boundary.

Court size. An NBA and FIBA court is 28.65 by 15.24 metres (94 by 50 feet). The rim sits 3.05 metres (10 feet) above the floor, mounted on a backboard.

The three-point line. Its distance from the hoop varies slightly by level and league — roughly 6.75 metres in FIBA and international play, and 7.24 metres at the top of the arc in the NBA — but its function is the same everywhere: reward longer, harder shots with an extra point.

The ball. A men's basketball is size 7 (about 749–780 grams, roughly 75–76 cm in circumference); a women's basketball is size 6, slightly smaller and lighter. Balls are made of leather or a synthetic composite with a pebbled surface for grip.

Other equipment. Players wear lightweight jerseys and shorts, high-top or low-top athletic shoes built for quick lateral movement, and often ankle or knee supports. A shot clock, game clock, and scoreboard are standard at every organised level of play.

Positions on the floor

Traditional basketball uses five positions, numbered one through five, running roughly from smallest and most ball-skilled to tallest and closest to the hoop. Modern teams blend these roles far more than they used to, but the basic framework still shapes how teams are built.

  • 1
    Point guardUsually the smallest, quickest player and the primary ball-handler, responsible for organising plays and setting up teammates.
  • 2
    Shooting guardOften the team's most accurate outside shooter, expected to score from mid-range and beyond the three-point line.
  • 3
    Small forwardA versatile wing player who scores, rebounds, and defends across multiple positions.
  • 4
    Power forwardA strong interior player who rebounds and scores close to the basket, and increasingly shoots from outside as well.
  • 5
    CentreTypically the tallest player on the floor, anchoring defence near the rim and finishing plays close to the basket.
05

Core skills and techniques

Every player, regardless of position, builds their game around the same five fundamentals.

Dribbling

Bouncing the ball with one hand while moving, used to advance the ball and evade defenders. Good dribblers keep the ball low and use both hands equally.

Shooting

Releasing the ball toward the hoop with control and arc. Form typically involves balanced footwork, a straight elbow, and a soft wrist follow-through.

Passing

Moving the ball between teammates using chest passes, bounce passes, and overhead passes to create better scoring opportunities than one player could alone.

Defense

Positioning the body between the opponent and the basket, contesting shots and passing lanes without making illegal contact.

Rebounding

Gaining possession of the ball after a missed shot, on either the offensive or defensive end, using timing and positioning as much as height.

Footwork

The pivoting, cutting, and stopping techniques that let a player move, guard, and shoot without committing a violation.

06

Leagues and global competition

Basketball's professional and international structure spans nearly every continent, with a handful of competitions that define the sport at the highest level.

NBA

National Basketball Association

The top men's professional league in the United States and Canada, founded in 1946 and widely regarded as the strongest league in the world by playing talent.

WNBA

Women's National Basketball Association

The premier women's professional league in the United States, founded in 1996 and played during the northern-hemisphere summer.

FIBA

International Basketball Federation

The global governing body that sets the official rules for international play and organises the Basketball World Cup and Olympic tournaments.

NCAA

College basketball

University-level competition in the United States, culminating each spring in the single-elimination tournament widely known as "March Madness."

Euro

EuroLeague

The top-tier professional club competition in Europe, drawing teams from multiple countries each season.

Olympics

Olympic basketball

Held every four years; the men's tournament has run since 1936 and the women's tournament since 1976.

08

Health benefits of playing

Basketball combines aerobic and anaerobic exercise, making it an efficient full-body workout as well as a team sport.

  • Cardiovascular fitness: The constant mix of running, sprinting, and jumping raises heart rate and builds endurance over time.
  • Coordination and balance: Dribbling, pivoting, and shooting develop hand-eye coordination and body control.
  • Bone and muscle strength: As a weight-bearing sport involving jumping and lateral movement, it helps build bone density and leg and core strength.
  • Social and mental benefits: As a team sport, it builds communication and cooperation skills, and regular play is commonly associated with reduced stress and improved mood.
  • Accessible entry point: A single hoop and ball are enough to practise many of the sport's basic skills alone, making it easier to pick up than sports that require more equipment or players.

As with any high-impact sport, warming up, using supportive footwear, and learning proper landing technique after jumps can reduce the risk of common injuries such as ankle sprains and knee strain.

Glossary of court terms

A short reference to terms you'll hear in almost any broadcast or pickup game.

Assist
A pass that directly leads to a teammate scoring.
Rebound
Gaining possession of the ball after a missed shot.
Turnover
Losing possession of the ball without attempting a shot, through a violation, steal, or bad pass.
Steal
Taking the ball away from the opposing team without fouling.
Pick and roll
A common play where one player blocks (screens) a defender for a teammate, then moves toward the basket to receive a pass.
Zone defense
A defensive scheme where players guard an area of the court rather than a specific opponent.
Man-to-man
A defensive scheme where each defender is responsible for guarding one specific opponent.
Fast break
A quick offensive move down the court before the defense can get organised, usually after a steal or rebound.
Air ball
A shot that misses the rim, backboard, and net entirely.
Double-double
A game in which a player reaches double digits in two statistical categories, such as points and rebounds.
Screen
Legally standing in a defender's path to free up a teammate.
Free throw
An unguarded shot from the free-throw line, awarded after certain fouls, worth one point.
