From flint tools
to artificial intelligence

Every era leaves behind the tools it built. This page traces how humans went from shaping stone to shaping silicon, and what each leap in technology actually changed.

Technology is rarely one invention — it's a chain of small improvements, each one making the next one possible. This page walks that chain, one link at a time.

What this page covers

Major categories of technology, key inventions, a timeline of turning points, and the people behind them — no products, no pitches.

Who it's for

Anyone curious about how today's devices trace back to much older, much simpler ideas.

How to read it

Start with the categories, move through key inventions, then follow the timeline from the first tools to the present day.

// 01 — CATEGORIES

Three broad families of technology

Most inventions in history fall into one of a few broad jobs: moving things, moving information, or doing calculations.

Transportation

From the wheel to the steam locomotive to the jet engine — technology that shrinks distance and moves people and goods faster.

Communication

From written language to the telegraph to the internet — technology that carries ideas across distance and time.

Computation

From the abacus to the transistor to modern AI — technology that processes information and, increasingly, makes decisions.

// 02 — KEY INVENTIONS

Inventions that changed the rules

A handful of breakthroughs did more than improve on what came before — they opened up possibilities nobody had access to yet.

Communication · c. 1440

The printing press

Johannes Gutenberg's movable-type press made copying text fast and cheap for the first time in history. Books that once took months to hand-copy could now be produced in days, and literacy spread across Europe faster than any single ruler could have managed on their own.

Transportation & Industry · c. 1712–1800s

The steam engine

Thomas Newcomen's early design, later refined by James Watt, turned heat into reliable mechanical motion. It powered factories, trains, and ships, and effectively kicked off the Industrial Revolution — the first time machines, not muscle, drove mass production.

Communication · 1876

The telephone

Alexander Graham Bell's telephone turned sound itself into a signal that could travel down a wire. For the first time, two people in different cities could hear each other's actual voice in real time — a leap that the telegraph, fast as it was, could never match.

Computation · 1947

The transistor

Invented at Bell Labs, the transistor replaced bulky, fragile vacuum tubes with a tiny, reliable switch. It's the single component that made small, affordable electronics possible — every modern chip is, at its core, millions of transistors working together.

Communication · 1989

The World Wide Web

Tim Berners-Lee, working at CERN, proposed a system of linked "hypertext" documents accessible over the internet. It turned a network built for research institutions into something anyone could browse — and eventually, the backbone of modern life.

Communication & Computation · 2007

The smartphone

The modern smartphone folded a phone, a computer, a camera, and a map into one pocket-sized device. It changed how billions of people communicate, work, and navigate the world, often within just a few years of first arriving in a given country.

// 03 — TIMELINE

A short history of human tools

From the first sharpened stone to today's algorithms — the pace of change keeps accelerating.

c. 2,600,000 BCE

The first stone tools

Early humans began deliberately shaping stone into cutting and pounding tools — arguably the very first piece of technology, and one that stayed in use for over two million years.

c. 3500 BCE

The wheel

First used for pottery in Mesopotamia before being adapted for transport, the wheel remains one of the most influential mechanical inventions in history.

c. 1440

The printing press

Gutenberg's press made the mass production of written text possible for the first time, accelerating the spread of knowledge across Europe.

1712–1800s

The steam engine & the Industrial Revolution

Steam power moved production out of workshops and into factories, reshaping economies and cities within a single generation.

1879

The practical electric light bulb

Thomas Edison's long-lasting filament bulb made electric lighting viable for everyday homes, not just laboratories and workshops.

1946

The first general-purpose computer

ENIAC, built in the United States, filled an entire room and could be reprogrammed for different calculations — a defining first step toward modern computing.

1969

ARPANET, the internet's ancestor

A US government research network connected four university computers, laying the technical groundwork for the internet we use today.

Today

The age of artificial intelligence

Machine learning systems now write, translate, and generate images — the latest chapter in a very long story about tools that extend human ability.

// 04 — IMPACT

How technology reshapes daily life

Every major wave of technology tends to change more than one part of life at once.

What tends to improve

  • Access to information and education
  • Speed of travel and communication
  • Medical diagnosis and treatment
  • Productivity in farming and manufacturing
  • Connection across long distances

What tends to get harder

  • Jobs built around older tools disappearing
  • Managing privacy and personal data
  • Keeping rules and laws up to date
  • Unequal access between rich and poor regions
  • Environmental cost of manufacturing and energy use
// 05 — PEOPLE

A few names behind the inventions

Technology is usually credited to one name, even though it's almost always built on the work of many people before them.

Printing

Johannes Gutenberg

German inventor whose movable-type printing press, developed around 1440, is widely regarded as the starting point of the information age.

Electricity

Thomas Edison

American inventor credited with the practical incandescent light bulb and holder of over 1,000 patents across his career.

Computing

Alan Turing

British mathematician whose theoretical work laid the foundations of modern computer science, and whose codebreaking work shortened the Second World War.

Computing

Ada Lovelace

English mathematician often considered the first computer programmer, for her work on Charles Babbage's proposed Analytical Engine in the 1840s.

Internet

Tim Berners-Lee

British computer scientist who invented the World Wide Web in 1989, turning the internet into something the general public could actually use.

Personal computing

Steve Wozniak

American engineer who designed the Apple I and Apple II, helping bring the personal computer out of research labs and into ordinary homes.

// 06 — FACTS

Facts worth knowing

The first computer "bug" was, quite literally, a moth found stuck in a relay of the Harvard Mark II in 1947.

The word "robot" comes from the Czech word "robota," meaning forced labour, first used in a 1920 play.

Early telephone users had to ask a human operator to manually connect their call at a switchboard.

The first text message ever sent, in 1992, simply read "Merry Christmas."

The Wright brothers' first powered flight in 1903 covered less distance than the length of a modern Boeing 747.

The floppy disk symbol is still used as the universal "save" icon, despite the disk itself being decades obsolete.

The QWERTY keyboard layout was originally designed to slow typists down and prevent mechanical typewriters from jamming.

A modern smartphone has vastly more computing power than the guidance computers used on the Apollo moon missions.

// 07 — GLOSSARY

A short glossary of tech terms

Analog
A system that represents information as a continuously varying signal, rather than as discrete digits.
Bandwidth
The amount of data that can be sent through a connection in a given amount of time.
Open source
Software whose underlying code is made publicly available for anyone to view, use, or modify.
Prototype
An early working version of a product, built to test an idea before full production.
Obsolescence
The process by which a technology becomes outdated and is gradually replaced by something newer.
Patent
A legal right that gives an inventor exclusive control over their invention for a set period of time.
